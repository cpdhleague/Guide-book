The *Secret Lair* crossover between Magic and the Brooklyn art collective MSCHF has created some mischief of its own. The Zeta Set reprints 121 cards in the style of Magic's 1992 playtest cards — photocopied, hand-cut, and weird — and in doing so printed twelve cards at common for the first time and dropped a handful of creatures to uncommon.

Wizards and the Pauper Format Panel initially ruled the downshifts wouldn't affect Pauper legality. After a week of community feedback, they reversed course. [Gavin Verhey's full statement is worth reading](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/announcements/on-the-zeta-set-in-pauper).

The PDH Rules Committee does its best to align with rulings from both the Pauper and Commander formats to keep PDH accessible to those arriving from other areas of the Magic ecosystem. To stay in alignment, the committee has decided to implement all of the cards from the Zeta set to be legal in PDH, both commons and uncommons. They have not yet announced whether these inclusions will be arriving with any bans. They have communicated that they are still in discussion and review of these cards and have asked the community to give them time while they give these surprise inclusions due consideration before making any definitive announcements. Nothing here should be read as a prediction of what they'll decide. These are hypothetical competitive observations, not petitions.

This article is going to look at the strongest of these cards and their potential impact competitively. We're only covering cards we expect to see competitive play, so if your favorite Zeta card isn't here, that's not a knock on it. It's very cool that the iconic [[Serra Angel]] is now legal in the 99 of our decks, but it won't be seeing a ton of competitive play.

## The Commanders

Eight creatures came down to uncommon for the first time in this set. Four of them are worth talking about competitively. They're ranked here from least to most likely to make a dent in cPDH.

### 4. Obeka, Brute Chronologist

{% include card-grid.html cards="Obeka, Brute Chronologist;" %}

[[Obeka, Brute Chronologist]] is a favorite of mine in EDH, but the port to PDH doesn't bring the splashier effects from non-common cards that Obeka usually uses to get infinite turns or make obscene numbers of tokens. The fun part of Obeka is that ending the turn wipes the stack and skips every delayed trigger that was waiting for end of combat or end of turn. PDH is full of cool effects, but some really strong ones have drawbacks. Obeka gets rid of the drawbacks.

Here's what that opens up, roughly in order of how excited I am about it.

**Myriad** is the headliner, and it might be the best thing Obeka does in this format. Myriad tokens are exiled at end of combat — unless the turn simply ends first, in which case they stick around permanently. The tokens are copies, which means they have myriad too. That's exponential growth each turn! Unfortunately there are only two myriad creatures in Grixis.

**Token production with a sacrifice clause** works the same way. Mobilize gives you creatures that leave at end of combat; [[Mordor Trebuchet]] does something similar if you're attacking with a Goblin or Orc.

**Temporary creatures become permanent ones.** Warp, Unearth, Encore, Evoke, Blitz, Dash, and Decayed all lend you something with the intent that it'll only be temporary. 

**Entry costs and upkeep costs go away.** Bouncelands want to return a land to your hand; [[Command Bridge]], [[Ruptured Spire]], and [[Gateway Plaza]] want a payment or a sacrifice. Those are all triggers, and triggers on the stack cease to exist when the turn ends suddenly.

One idea worth checking out might be self-milling until we can cast [[Dragon's Approach]] to tutor into [[Tiamat's Fanatics]], cheating our favorite card straight into play and beginning the token explosion across our board.

[[Freed from the Real]] lets you activate Obeka multiple times per turn cycle, which does something subtle and mean — it discourages the whole table from committing to end-step plays, because you can simply work with your opponents to exile those effects, spells, and activations.

{% include card-grid.html cards="Dragon's Approach; Tiamat's Fanatics; Ideas Unbound;" %}

I'll be building it, and there are some terrific ways to abuse Obeka's abilities.


### 3. The Jolly Balloon Man

{% include card-grid.html cards="The Jolly Balloon Man;" %}

In EDH, [[The Jolly Balloon Man]] is a combo engine — roughly twelve thousand decks on EDHREC, and Commander Spellbook lists over a thousand distinct combos involving it. Give it a way to untap and you get infinite hasty token copies, infinite ETB triggers, infinite everything.

There are known combos here that can be put together. The cleanest one: Jolly Balloon Man plus [[Village Bell-Ringer]] plus any creature that taps for {1}. Tap your mana creature, use the Balloon Man to copy the Bell-Ringer, and the token's enter trigger untaps your whole board — including the Balloon Man and the mana creature. Do it again. That's infinite hasty creatures, infinite enter-the-battlefield triggers, and infinite untaps, off three commons. Throw an [[Impact Tremors]] into the mix and you've got yourself a win. 

The problem here isn't the combo. It's that we don't have the tutors or the resources to find those pieces consistently enough to reliably close out games before our opponents can. Jolly Balloon Man has the potential to be very strong, but it would have to use its copying ability in some way that stalls the game or draws cards to reliably close out matches.

It's like we have the perfect starting point, and the perfect destination to arrive at, but no road leading from point A to point B. Kudos to the brewers that figure out how to stall or dig deep enough to make lots of happy balloons (don't mind their faces).

### 2. Garth One-Eye

{% include card-grid.html cards="Garth One-Eye;" %}

[[Garth One-Eye]] has made his rounds in EDH and cEDH — the "make a Black Lotus" commander has appeal both to casual players who want to play with copies of the banned card and combo players ready to make infinite mana to win their games. Competitive builds lean on blink and haste effects to reuse his ability, generating infinite mana off repeated Lotuses. [[Displacer Kitten]], [[Deadeye Navigator]], and haste enablers are the usual suspects.

Brewers in our Discord are already circling this one, and I understand the excitement. Garth offers a wealth of control pieces and value at instant speed, and as a five-color commander he has access to every common in the format. That last part is not a small thing.

What he has to contend with is being five CMC with five different pips, and then executing somewhat convoluted combo lines to win games. There are plenty of lines that can be run here, but they're convoluted. Convoluted can sometimes mean losing to the deck that's already pushing for a win on turn four or five.

One potential: Garth might not need to combo at all. Because he offers so much raw utility, we may see our first real five-color good-stuff deck — a list that functions entirely without its commander, with Garth in the command zone as a mixed bag of support.

More likely I think Garth will need to play a control plan for the early-to-mid game until it has the mana and resources to tutor for its pieces and win. Will he be able to do it all in time, or hold off his opponents while he does so? Let's find out. 

### 1. Raffine, Scheming Seer

{% include card-grid.html cards="Raffine, Scheming Seer;" %}

[[Raffine, Scheming Seer]] was a genuine powerhouse in Standard and carved out significant roles in Duel Commander, EDH, and Historic Brawl. Where it didn't land was the faster eternal formats — Pioneer and Modern largely passed on it, because three distinct color pips and a plan that wants a board are hard asks when those formats are trying to kill you on turn three.

PDH sits in a friendlier spot. Even competitive PDH gives you more room than Modern does (most of the time), and Raffine's connive triggers will likely find a quick home here.

What makes Raffine dangerous is that it allows a variety of approaches to winning games. You can go wide. You can Voltron. Or, because you're cycling through so many cards, you can find combo pieces the way [[Horrid Shadowspinner]] does. Speaking of which, if the RC leaves Raffine to enter the format untouched, Horrid Shadowspinner may have seen its last days. With an additional color, built in evasion, and minor protection, there's a lot going for Raffine.

This is the one most likely to go nuts right away. It comes ready-made in some ways, with so much research done on Horrid Shadowspinner to support the gameplan. I'd be surprised not to see Raffine appearing at tournaments pretty soon and coming ready to dominate.

## The Commons

Five of the six commons we're going to mention here today are interaction. Let's jump in.

### An Offer You Can't Refuse

{% include card-grid.html cards="An Offer You Can't Refuse;" %}

One-mana counterspells are rare and very good for PDH. We've seen this card's flexibility in cEDH, where it doubles as a pseudo-ritual — you can give yourself the two treasures in a pinch, or at instant speed before your next turn. 

Good cards are good. What more can we say?

### Dispatch and Cut Down

{% include card-grid.html cards="Dispatch; Cut Down;" %}

[[Dispatch]] is a welcome card and an excellent removal tool in white — a color that wants more cheap removal. Most decks can run an artifact land or two, plus their normal array of mana rocks to turn Dispatch into a [[Swords to Plowshares]].

[[Cut Down]] is another removal spell for black, very cheap but also a bit limited due to the power/toughness restrictions.

These are nice, cheap forms of removal, but nothing too startling or extraordinary. They'll see play. 

### Soul-Guide Lantern

{% include card-grid.html cards="Soul-Guide Lantern;" %}

Very similar to existing cards like [[Relic of Progenitus]], so while we'll definitely be seeing more of it, it's not going to change things all too significantly. In fact, it's a downgrade to Relic — so decks looking to run only one of this effect will probably be turning to older cards first.

### Vandalblast

{% include card-grid.html cards="Vandalblast;" %}

[[Vandalblast]] is a bit of an interesting card, and it'd be the first of its kind in our format if it stays legal.

A good variety of artifact strategies don't have counterspells or the resources to rebuild after a wipe. Graveyard decks like [[Hollow Marauder]] have lived with comparable hate for a while, though refilling a graveyard is generally easier than rebuilding a board of artifacts. An artifact wipe would be devastating to decks that have built their entire plans around artifacts. 

Even outside of artifact strats, artifact ramp is abundant. Non-green decks lean on artifact mana to reach expensive commanders and bigger spells. Vandalblast doesn't invalidate that, but it adds a real sense of risk to it. Not every deck running artifacts will have blue to be able to interact with Vandalblast on the stack. 

Fortunately, Vandalblast often doesn't further a player's own gameplan, so many of our faster decks may not be inclined to include it. It's also difficult to tutor — the most consistent route is [[Dizzy Spell]], which means being in Izzet colors. So it may be that we don't actually see a lot of it in cPDH, where combo is currently outpacing most other strategies. It may be that the card is simply too inconsistently available to truly shut down abundant ramp or artifact-based gameplans, and will have more impact on casual play.

### The Price of Progress Question

{% include card-grid.html cards="Price of Progress;" %}

[[Price of Progress]] is an interesting arrival. In certain decks, like an efficient [[Glaring Fleshraker]], you may be incentivized to run lots of lands with abilities that also make your colorless mana. Same for 3- and 5-color decks looking to fix their mana. How does Price of Progress affect these decks? Colorless decks certainly won't be able to stop this card on the stack. Multicolored decks would be more likely to have blue in their mix, but it's still a scary threat to be staring down when you'd been holding up interaction for a [[Malcolm, Keen-Eyed Navigator]].

On the other hand, maybe there aren't enough nonbasics out there to affect things in the way I think there are. There are a lot of top decks in the meta right now running few nonbasic lands.

It's a strong card, and it requires very little build around from the pilots bringing it to the table. It'll be interesting to discover whether it makes a big impact on existing decks or makes decks that might want or need to rely on a variety of lands more difficult to build since they also need to consider the potential of getting exploded for big damage unexpectedly sometimes.

## What Happens Next

We're waiting to hear final word on which of these cards will be legal in PDH, but since the committee has long been oriented toward keeping the banlist as small as possible, I'm of the hope that we see all of these cards in action — at least for some time — before we have any bans.

For us, the practical takeaway is that there are new decks to build. Raffine is ready to go. Garth is a puzzle worth solving. There's new interaction to fit into existing lists, and a couple of cards that might change how you think about your manabase.

Whatever the rulings, we're excited to see new cards, new potential, and how these surprise adjustments shake up the meta.
